Unconfoundedness vs Parallel Trends

Which assumption fits your self-selected treatment? Same data, same estimator, two different answers. The assumption decides which one is right.

Most treatments in product analytics are chosen by the user. Someone turns on a feature, joins a community, or starts a free trial of a premium tier. There is no randomisation, but under the right assumptions we can still estimate the effect of these self-selected treatments. Without a valid instrument or a cut-off to exploit (the settings for instrumental variables or regression discontinuity), analysts tend to fall into one of two camps:

  • Lagged-outcome regression, usually estimated with double machine learning. This camp learned from Causal Inference for the Brave and True and uses libraries like EconML and CausalML.
  • Difference-in-differences, learned from The Mixtape and brought up to date by papers like Sant'Anna and Zhao's Doubly Robust Difference-in-Differences Estimators (2020).

Both are reasonable, and both can be estimated with the same doubly robust machinery. DoubleML implements both. That is the point this article is built around: the estimator and the identifying assumption are separate choices, and the assumption decides whether the answer is right.

  • Lagged-outcome regression assumes no unobserved confounding once you condition on past outcomes and covariates.
  • Difference-in-differences assumes parallel trends: without treatment, treated and control outcomes would have moved together.

This simulation study shows when each assumption fails and gives a rule for choosing between them.

1 · Two designs

Lagged outcomes and difference-in-differences

Let's look more closely at what lagged-outcome regression and difference-in-differences each do.

Lagged-outcome regression compares treated and control users who had the same engagement history and characteristics before treatment. After reweighting so those histories match, the difference in post-period engagement is the effect.

Difference-in-differences lets the two groups start at different levels. It assumes the gap between them would have stayed the same without treatment, so any change in the gap after treatment is the effect.

Each design is a choice of assumption, not of estimator. Lagged-outcome regression can be estimated by regression adjustment, inverse propensity weighting, matching or double machine learning. Difference-in-differences has the same menu, from two-way fixed effects to the doubly robust estimator of Sant'Anna and Zhao. In practice DiD is usually conditional: parallel trends only need to hold among users with the same characteristics, measured before the pre-period. Pre-period outcomes are the exception. Conditioning on them pulls DiD towards the other design and its weaknesses, which section 4 covers.

The estimator cannot rescue a wrong assumption. A doubly robust estimator is robust to misspecifying the outcome or propensity model, not to choosing the wrong design.

A useful way to see the difference is to write the post-period outcome as a function of the pre-period outcome:

Ypost = α + τD + β Ypre + γ′X + ε

Here D is the treatment, τ the effect and X the observed covariates.

  • Difference-in-differences fixes β = 1. It subtracts each user's whole pre-period level, so anything that is stable within a user cancels out.
  • Unconfoundedness lets the data estimate β. If engagement is partly transient, β is below 1 and only part of an unusually low pre-period carries forward.

Each design breaks where its β is wrong. That gives two failure modes.

2 · Failure modes

Two failure modes

Each design rests on an assumption about why users opted in. When the real reason breaks that assumption, the estimate is biased, and nothing in the estimator warns you.

Failure mode 1: a stable trait you cannot see

Some users are more engaged than others week after week, for reasons that never appear in your data. Think of how well the product fits someone's workflow. Users for whom it is a poor fit log in less every week, and they are also more likely to try new features in search of something that works. That trait drives both who opts in and how engaged they are, and you cannot measure it. It is an unobserved confounder.

DiD handles this. A trait that adds the same amount to every week disappears when you subtract the pre-period. Lagged-outcome regression only handles it as well as the lagged outcomes reveal the trait. Eight noisy weeks are an imperfect proxy, so the model reads part of the gap as noise and predicts the treated users drifting towards the controls.

The treated users sit lower every week because of a trait the data does not contain. The lagged-outcome prediction drifts towards the controls, so the estimated effect is too small.

Failure mode 2: selection on a temporary dip

Users often opt in when something has just changed. Take a win-back campaign: users whose activity has slipped are offered a free trial of a premium tier, and the ones who accept are disproportionately in a temporary slump. Many of them would have bounced back anyway. Ashenfelter (1978) found the same pattern in job training: trainees' earnings fall just before they enrol and recover afterwards, whether the training worked or not.

Lagged-outcome regression compares dipped treated users with controls who dipped the same way, and both groups recover. DiD subtracts a pre-period depressed by the dip, so the recovery shows up as part of the effect.

The treated users opt in at the bottom of a dip and would have recovered anyway. DiD projects the dipped level forward, so the recovery is counted as part of the effect.

The same thing happens in reverse. A user in an unusually busy patch, say around a project deadline, is more likely to switch on power features, and their engagement would have fallen back afterwards. Selection on a temporary spike makes DiD understate the effect instead. The simulation uses a dip, but the lesson holds either way: DiD fails whenever users opt in during a temporary departure from their normal level.

3 · The simulation

A simulation with a known answer

To test this we need data where the true effect is known. The simulation builds a weekly panel of 10,000 users. Each user's engagement has four parts: a level set by observed covariates and a hidden stable trait; a trend that depends on the observed covariates; shared seasonality; and a transitory shock that decays by 20% a week. Every user gets a candidate week. Treated users opt in that week, and controls are users who were eligible that week and did not.

The treatment adds exactly 2.0 to weekly engagement. Both potential outcomes are simulated for every user, so the true ATT is known. What changes between scenarios is why users opt in. In every scenario, users with lower engagement are more likely to opt in.

4 · Covariates

Lagged outcomes vs DiD covariate rules

The two designs need different covariates, and mixing them up quietly changes the assumption.

  • Unconfoundedness: condition on the outcome history. Here that is engagement in each of the eight weeks before opt-in, its slope and the most recent change, plus the non-outcome covariates.
  • DiD: use non-outcome covariates measured before the pre-period. Do not add outcome lags from the pre-period.

The second rule surprises people. Adding a few early lags to a DiD feels like a harmless robustness check. Daw and Hatfield (2018) showed it is not: when treated and control groups differ in level, matching them on pre-period outcomes pairs each treated user with controls whose outcomes were temporarily unusual, and those controls then regress to their own mean.

To measure the cost, the study includes a third design, DiD with lags: the DiD from above with engagement at weeks -8 to -5 added as covariates.

5 · The diagnostic

Event-time plots: pre-trends and placebo

If the two designs fail in different situations, the useful question is which situation you are in. The pre-period is the only place to look, since nothing has been treated there yet. It has to be used carefully.

The usual check is covariate balance after weighting. It always passes, because the weights were fitted to balance exactly those features, including in scenario b where the design is biased. A real check has to look at something the propensity model did not see.

The DiD check: pre-trends

For DiD, the assumption is about trends, so test the trend before week 0. Weight controls on the non-outcome covariates only, as the DiD design does, and express each user's engagement relative to their own average over weeks -8 to -5. That removes stable level differences. Under parallel trends the gap between treated and control should be flat at zero over weeks -4 to -1.

The unconfoundedness check: a placebo on the history

For unconfoundedness, move the whole design back in time. Fit the propensity model on the history through week -5 only and look at weeks -4 to -1. Nothing has been treated, so any gap means selection on the observed history did not explain who opted in.

Unconfoundedness check: propensity on history through week -5
Treated minus weighted control, 95% bands

In b the gap drifts down slowly. In c and d it drops sharply in the last three weeks.

In b the gap drifts: matching on four noisy early weeks leaves a trait difference that keeps showing up. That drift is the signature of a hidden stable trait.

In c the placebo fails too, even though unconfoundedness holds at week 0. Selection in c runs on engagement in weeks -3 to -1, and a placebo that stops at week -5 drops exactly those weeks. So no pre-period check can separate "users opted in after a dip" (c) from "users opted in after a dip and have a lower hidden trait" (d).

The decision rule

DiD pre-trend checkUnconfoundedness placeboWhat to do
flatflatBoth designs are plausible and should agree. Report either.
flatdriftsA stable hidden trait. Use DiD.
dipsflat or dipsParallel trends fails. Use unconfoundedness with lags, and report DiD as the other bound.

The last row needs a word of explanation. A dip rules out DiD, but the placebo cannot rule out a stable trait on top of the dip. Ding and Li (2019), building on Angrist and Pischke, show that under conditions like these the two estimates bracket the truth: with negative selection, unconfoundedness errs low and DiD errs high. Reporting both gives a range that holds whichever of c or d you are in.

Pick the design from the pre-period data, not from the textbook you learned on. When the data cannot decide, report both estimates as bounds.

The repo wraps both checks in one function. On your own panel:

from uvpt.diagnostics import diagnose
from uvpt.features import to_wide

wide = to_wide(panel, user="user_id", time="event_time", treated="treated",
               outcome="sessions", covariates=["tenure", "platform"], week="week")
out = diagnose(wide)
print(out["reading"])

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Why not include every covariate in both designs, just to be safe?

Because covariates are part of the assumption. Outcome lags make unconfoundedness plausible, and the same lags break DiD when the groups differ in level. Section 4 shows DiD with lags is as biased as unconfoundedness in scenario b.

Can I use DiD when the outcome is churn?

Not directly. Churn is absorbing, and a user has to be active to be offered a feature, so pre-period churn is zero for everyone in the analysis. That causes three problems. There is no pre-trend to check. Subtracting zero from both groups leaves the naive comparison of post-period churn. And the sample is filtered on survival, which can treat the groups differently if opting in depends on recent engagement.

For churn, condition on the history instead: unconfoundedness with lagged engagement as covariates, or a survival model with time-varying covariates. This article uses weekly engagement, which is not absorbing, so both designs can be compared on the same data.

Does the choice of machine learning model matter?

Much less than the design. Swapping LightGBM for scikit-learn's HistGradientBoosting moves the estimates in scenarios b and c by at most 0.05, against design biases of 0.7 to 1.0. The flexible models matter for getting the nuisance functions right, and they cannot fix an assumption that does not hold.

Can sensitivity analysis tell me which design to trust?

No. DoubleML includes the omitted variable bias bounds of Chernozhukov et al., which ask how strong a hidden confounder would need to be to move the estimate. They are worth reporting, but they measure fragility to unobserved confounding and cannot see a broken parallel trends assumption. In scenario c the biased DiD estimate has a robustness value of 0.25, higher than the valid unconfoundedness estimate at 0.19. The pre-period checks are what choose the design.

What about overlap?

The ATT weights each control by p/(1-p), so controls who look like typical treated users carry a lot of weight, and when selection is strong a few can dominate. In scenario d the effective sample size of the control weights for the unconfoundedness design falls to about 700 out of 8,000 controls. Nothing needed clipping here, but check the weights on real data before trusting the interval.

What if users opt in at different times?

They do here: every user has their own index week, and controls are aligned to the week they were eligible. With staggered adoption and a shared calendar, use an estimator built for it, such as Callaway and Sant'Anna. The two failure modes and the pre-period checks carry over.

How should controls get an index date?

In this study, each control's index week is the week they were eligible and did not opt in. A common shortcut is to assign controls random dates drawn from the treated calendar. In scenario c that biased the unconfoundedness estimate by about 0.1, because the decision not to opt in was made in a different week from the one being compared.

What if I only have a few weeks of history?

Then neither check has much to work with. With fewer than about six pre-period weeks there is no room for a placebo window and a reference window, and the lags are a weaker proxy for any stable trait. Report both designs and say the data cannot choose between them.

Is synthetic control an alternative?

Synthetic control is built for a few treated units with long histories, such as regions or countries. With thousands of users each opting in at their own time, the designs here are the more natural fit. Its weighting on pre-period outcomes also makes it closer to unconfoundedness in spirit, so it shares that design's weakness to a hidden trait.

Bringing it together

The assumption is the decision

Both designs used equally sophisticated estimators. Which one was right depended only on whether its assumption matched how users chose to opt in.

  • Separate the estimator from the design. DML is a way to estimate. Unconfoundedness and parallel trends are what make the estimate mean something.
  • Match the covariates to the design. Lags belong in unconfoundedness and out of DiD.
  • Test the assumption, not the weights. Balance is mechanical. Pre-trends and placebos are not.
  • Report bounds when the data cannot choose. A dip before opt-in rules out DiD, and cannot rule out a hidden trait.
