Difference-in-Differences vs Lagged-Outcome Regression

A simulation study and a pre-period check for choosing the right design for self-selected treatments in product analytics.

Ryan O'Sullivan · Code and notebook on GitHub

Most treatments in product analytics are chosen by the user. Someone turns on a feature, joins a community, or starts a free trial of a premium tier. There is no randomisation, but under the right assumptions we can still estimate the effect of these self-selected treatments. Without a valid instrument or a cut-off to exploit (the settings for instrumental variables or regression discontinuity), analysts tend to fall into one of two camps:

  • Lagged-outcome regression, usually estimated with double machine learning. This camp learned from Causal Inference for the Brave and True and uses libraries like EconML and CausalML.
  • Difference-in-differences, learned from The Mixtape and brought up to date by papers like Sant'Anna and Zhao's Doubly Robust Difference-in-Differences Estimators (2020).

Both are reasonable, and both can be estimated with the same doubly robust machinery (DoubleML, for example, implements both). That is the point this article is built around: the estimator and the identifying assumption are separate choices, and the assumption decides whether the answer is right.

  • Lagged-outcome regression assumes unconfoundedness: no unobserved confounding once you condition on past outcomes and covariates.
  • Difference-in-differences assumes parallel trends: without treatment, treated and control outcomes would have moved together.

This simulation study shows when each assumption fails and gives a rule for choosing between them.

1. Lagged outcomes and difference-in-differences

Lagged-outcome regression compares treated and control users who had the same engagement history and characteristics before treatment. After re-weighting or adjusting so those histories match, the difference in post-period engagement is the effect.

Difference-in-differences lets the two groups start at different levels. It assumes the gap between them would have stayed the same without treatment, so any change in the gap after treatment is the effect. In practice DiD is usually conditional: parallel trends only need to hold among users with the same characteristics, measured before the pre-period.

Lagged-outcome regression can be estimated by regression adjustment, inverse propensity weighting or double machine learning. Difference-in-differences has the same menu, from two-way fixed effects to doubly robust DiD.

The estimator cannot rescue a wrong assumption. A doubly robust estimator is robust to misspecifying the outcome or propensity model, not to its identifying assumption failing.

A useful way to see the difference is to write the post-period outcome as a function of the pre-period outcome:

Ypost = α + τD + β Ypre + γ′X + ε

Here D is the treatment, τ the effect and X the observed covariates. τ is the treated–control gap after adjusting for Ypre and X. This linear form is the standard way to compare the designs (Angrist and Pischke 2009, section 5.4; Ding and Li 2019). The estimators in this article are more flexible, but the intuition carries over.

  • Difference-in-differences fixes β = 1. Moving Ypre to the left-hand side gives a regression of each user's change on D. Anything constant within a user cancels out, and τ is the difference in average change between treated and control.
  • Lagged-outcome regression lets the data estimate β. If engagement is partly transient, β is below 1 and only part of an unusually low pre-period carries forward.

Each design breaks where its β is wrong. That gives two failure modes.

2. Two failure modes

Failure mode 1: a time-invariant confounder

Take a free trial of a premium tier. Higher-income users are more likely to start it, and they also tend to engage more every week. Income rarely changes over a few months and is usually not in your data. That makes it an unobserved, time-invariant confounder.

DiD handles this: a constant difference between users cancels when you subtract the pre-period. Lagged-outcome regression can only adjust for income as far as past engagement reveals it, so part of the difference ends up in the estimate.

Failure mode 2: selection on a temporary dip

Users often opt in when something has just changed. Take a win-back campaign: users whose activity has slipped are offered a free trial of a premium tier, and the ones who accept are disproportionately in a temporary slump. Many of them would have bounced back anyway. Ashenfelter (1978) found the same pattern in job training: trainees' earnings fall just before they enrol and recover afterwards, whether the training worked or not.

DiD subtracts each user's pre-period, and its covariates are measured before it, so nothing in the design accounts for the dip. The pre-period is depressed, and the recovery is counted as part of the effect. A dip that hits treated and control users alike, such as a holiday week, is not a problem, because the control change absorbs it. Lagged-outcome regression conditions on the dip itself: it compares dipped treated users with controls who dipped the same way.

To measure these biases, and to test whether the data reveals them, we need a known true effect.

3. A simulation with a known answer

The simulation builds a weekly panel of 10,000 users. Each user's weekly engagement (think minutes per week, around 30 for a typical user) is the sum of four parts:

  • a level set by observed covariates and an unobserved time-invariant confounder
  • a trend that depends on the observed covariates
  • seasonality shared by everyone
  • a temporary shock that fades by 20% a week

Every user gets a candidate week. Treated users opt in that week, and controls are users who were eligible that week and did not. The feature adds 2 minutes a week for every treated user, so the true effect is 2.0. Engagement with and without the feature is simulated for every user, which is what lets us plot the true counterfactual below.

What changes between scenarios is why users opt in. In every scenario, users with lower engagement are more likely to opt in.

Lagged-outcome regression handles the dip because it conditions on the lagged outcomes. So can't we fix DiD by adding lagged outcomes to it too?

4. Can lagged outcomes fix DiD?

Adding a few early lags to a DiD feels like a harmless robustness check. Daw and Hatfield (2018) showed it is not. When treated and control groups differ in level, matching them on past outcomes pairs each treated user with controls whose outcomes were temporarily unusual, and those controls then regress to their own mean. That is failure mode 1, brought into DiD.

To measure the cost, the study includes a third design, DiD with lags: the DiD from above with engagement in weeks -8 to -5 added as covariates. These weeks come before DiD's baseline window, so the problem is not timing. It is that they are outcomes.

  • So: the lags only help DiD when parallel trends already fails. You only use DiD when the pre-trend check below is flat, and there they can only hurt.

That gives one covariate rule per design:

  • Lagged-outcome regression: condition on the outcome history. Here that is engagement in each of the eight weeks before opt-in, its slope and the most recent change, plus the non-outcome covariates.
  • DiD: use non-outcome covariates measured before the baseline window, and no outcome lags.

5. The diagnostic: check the pre-trend

The two designs fail in different situations, so the question is which situation you are in. The pre-period is where to look: nothing has been treated yet, so any difference between treated and control there comes from selection (assuming users do not change behaviour in anticipation of opting in).

Covariate balance after weighting is not a test. The weights are fitted to balance those features, so it passes in scenario b too, where lagged-outcome regression is biased.

The pre-trend check

Parallel trends is an assumption about trends, so test the trend before week 0, in the way DiD sees the data:

  1. Take each user's average engagement in weeks -8 to -5 as their reference level, and subtract it from every later week. This removes level differences, as DiD does.
  2. Weight the controls on the non-outcome covariates only, as the DiD design does.
  3. Plot the treated minus weighted-control gap week by week. If parallel trends holds, it stays flat at zero through weeks -4 to -1.
Pre-trend check: gap in change since weeks -8 to -5
Treated minus covariate-weighted control, 95% bands. Grey: weeks -4 to -1. Orange: post-period.

The largest |z| across weeks -4 to -1 is shown under each panel. The check flags above 2.5.

Flat in a and b, a clear dip in c and d. Parallel trends fails exactly where DiD was biased. Across the replications the check flagged every run of c and d, and of a and of b. The 2.5 threshold keeps the false alarm rate near 5% across the four weeks.

A flat pre-trend is evidence for parallel trends, not proof. It says nothing about the weeks after opt-in, and pre-tests have limited power (Roth 2022). A similar placebo for lagged-outcome regression (fit on the history up to week -5, check weeks -4 to -1) flagged b, c and d alike, so it cannot tell a confounder from a dip and is left out of the rule.

6. The decision rule

  • Flat pre-trend → DiD. It copes with a time-invariant confounder, which lagged-outcome regression cannot.
  • Pre-trend dips → lagged-outcome regression, with DiD as the other bound. The dip rules out DiD, but nothing in the pre-period rules out a time-invariant confounder as well.
Pre-trend checkDiDLagged-outcome regressionReport
Flat✅⚠️ only if there is no time-invariant confounderDiD
Dips❌✅ unless there is also a time-invariant confounderLagged-outcome regression, with DiD as the other bound

Why do the two estimates work as bounds? If either assumption holds, plus a stationarity condition on the outcome, they bracket the truth (Ding and Li 2019, building on Angrist and Pischke). Here the treated users start lower, so lagged-outcome regression errs low and DiD errs high. With positive selection the directions flip. Scenario d, where both assumptions fail, is outside that result. The truth still fell between the two estimates in of replications, but that is a property of this simulation, not a guarantee.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use DiD when the outcome is churn?

Not directly. Churn is absorbing, and a user has to be active to be offered a feature, so pre-period churn is zero for everyone in the analysis. That causes three problems. There is no pre-trend to check. Subtracting zero from both groups leaves the naive comparison of post-period churn. And the sample is filtered on survival, which can treat the groups differently if opting in depends on recent engagement.

For churn, condition on the history instead: lagged-outcome regression, or a survival model with time-varying covariates. This article uses weekly engagement, which is not absorbing, so both designs can be compared on the same data.

Can sensitivity analysis tell me which design to trust?

No. DoubleML includes the omitted variable bias bounds of Chernozhukov et al. They say how strong a hidden confounder, of levels or of trends, would have to be to move the estimate, not whether one exists. In scenario c the biased DiD estimate looks more robust (robustness value 0.25) than the valid lagged-outcome estimate (0.19). The pre-trend check is what chooses the design.

The assumption is the decision

The same estimator was used in both designs. The right one was whichever assumption matched how users opted in.

  • Separate the estimator from the design. DML is a way to estimate. Unconfoundedness and parallel trends are what make it causal.
  • Let the pre-trend choose. Flat: DiD. Dip: lagged-outcome regression, with DiD as the other bound.
